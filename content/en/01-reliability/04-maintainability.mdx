# The Three Pillars of Maintainability

The chapters so far have covered "resistance to breaking" (reliability) and "coping
with load" (scalability). Maintainability, the subject of this chapter, is a somewhat
different kind of property — it's about how easy the system stays to work with over the
long stretch of time after it's built.

## Why maintainability matters

For most systems, the period spent operating and modifying the system after it's built
is far longer than the period spent building it in the first place. New features, bug
fixes, integrations with other systems, regulatory requirements — the reasons to change
a system never run out. A system with poor maintainability takes longer to change every
time, and that makes incidents more likely. Maintainability is easier to work with when
you break it into three independent (yet related) properties.

## <Term slug="operability">Operability</Term>: keeping the system easy to run

Operability is the property that makes day-to-day operational work — monitoring,
incident response, configuration changes, deployment — easy to carry out. Concretely,
the following elements strengthen operability.

- The system's internal state can be observed from the outside (logs, metrics, traces)
- Behavior is predictable, with few unexpected side effects
- Manual work is minimized, and repeated tasks are easy to automate
- Runbooks describing what to do when something goes wrong are kept up to date

Much of the "protection against human error" discussed in the previous chapter is
itself an effort to strengthen operability. A system that's easy to operate ends up
having fewer failures caused by human error as a result.

## <Term slug="simplicity">Simplicity</Term>: reducing complexity

Simplicity means reducing complexity that doesn't stem from the inherent difficulty of
the problem the system solves. A given system may deal with a problem that's
inherently complex, and that can't be avoided — but complexity introduced by the
particulars of an implementation or design (tangled dependencies, layers of ad hoc
exception handling piled on top of each other) can be reduced.

<Callout type="info">
The primary tool for reducing complexity is good abstraction. Hiding shared logic
behind a clean interface means callers don't need to think about the details, which
keeps the codebase easier to navigate as a whole. That said, stacking too many layers
of abstraction on top of each other turns the layers themselves into a new source of
complexity, so this needs to be used with care.
</Callout>

## <Term slug="evolvability">Evolvability</Term>: staying easy to change

Evolvability is the property that lets a system be changed safely and cheaply as future
requirements shift. Requirements are rarely fully known up front — they tend to reveal
themselves gradually as the system is operated. Because of that, whether a system is
structured to "make the next change easy" — not just "meet today's requirements" — has
a large effect on long-term cost.

Keeping modules loosely coupled and being able to verify the blast radius of a change
through tests are both things that strengthen evolvability. Where simplicity focuses on
"reducing complexity now," evolvability focuses on "staying easy to change in the
future" — the two are complementary.

## Summing up the three pillars

Reliability, scalability, and maintainability (operability, simplicity, and
evolvability) are themes this course returns to again and again. When you study
individual techniques like replication or partitioning, keeping the question "is this
for reliability, for scalability, or for maintainability?" in mind makes it much easier
to hold onto why that technique exists in the first place.
