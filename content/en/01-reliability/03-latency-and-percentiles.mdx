# Latency and Percentiles

The previous chapter noted that averages alone aren't enough when talking about
performance. This chapter looks concretely at why averages are so easy to misread, and
how to work with the distribution of response times, using
<Term slug="percentile">percentiles</Term> as the tool.

## Looking at the distribution of <Term slug="latency">latency</Term>

If you measure how long an operation takes hundreds or thousands of times, the
measurements don't collapse to a single value — they form a distribution. Most requests
tend to complete at a similar speed, while a small fraction take an extremely long
time, producing a long-tailed distribution.

Taking the arithmetic mean of such a distribution gets pulled by that small fraction of
extremely slow requests, and tends to produce a number that doesn't represent "how fast
a typical user's experience is." If what you want to know is how slow things feel for
most users, you need to look at the shape of the distribution itself.

## The idea behind percentiles

A percentile is a measure of where the boundary falls when you sort measured values
from smallest to largest and ask "what fraction of values fall at or below this point."

- **p50 (median)**: half of all requests complete at or below this value
- **p95**: 95% of requests complete at or below this value (put differently, 1 in 20
  requests is slower than this)
- **p99**: 99% of requests complete at or below this value (1 in 100 requests is slower
  than this)

p50 represents "typical speed," while p95 and p99 represent "how long the slow tail
stretches." When discussing a service's perceived quality, looking at p95 and p99
alongside p50 keeps you from missing situations where "most users have a comfortable
experience, but a subset of users is clearly being made to wait."

<Callout type="tip">
Defining an SLA (service level agreement) as "p99 under 500ms" rather than "average
response time under 200ms" is a common practice precisely because it guarantees the
slow-tail experience that an average would hide.
</Callout>

## <Term slug="tail-latency-amplification">Tail latency amplification</Term>

Percentiles matter even more once you consider architectures where a single
user-facing request triggers multiple backend calls in parallel (fan-out) behind the
scenes.

In that case, the response time the user experiences matches whichever of the parallel
calls was slowest. Even if each individual call's p99 is fast enough on its own, once a
single request depends on 10 parallel calls, the probability that at least one of those
10 calls draws a slow outlier is much higher than it would be for a single call alone.
The more calls you make in parallel, the more the distribution of latency users
actually experience deteriorates relative to the percentile of any single dependency on
its own. This phenomenon is tail latency amplification.

<Callout type="warn">
"Every dependency has a fast enough p99, so we're fine" is an easy trap to fall into
in architectures with fan-out. It's not enough to check each dependency's percentile in
isolation — you also need to check how the combined effect changes the latency
experienced across the whole request.
</Callout>
