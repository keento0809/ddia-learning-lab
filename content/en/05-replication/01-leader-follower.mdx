# Leader/Follower Replication Basics

Keeping copies of the same data on multiple machines is called replication. This lesson
focuses on the most widely used setup in practice — leader/follower replication — and looks
at why we replicate data in the first place, and at what point a copy gets treated as
"confirmed."

## Why replicate data at all

There are three main motivations for replicating data.

1. **Availability**: if one machine fails or goes offline, another machine holding a copy
   can keep serving requests.
2. **Lower latency across regions**: placing a replica closer to users avoids round trips to
   a distant data center.
3. **Scaling out read throughput**: spreading read requests across several replicas lets a
   system handle more reads than a single machine could serve alone.

Wanting writes concentrated on one place while wanting reads spread out is exactly the tension
that makes the leader-based setup described next so common.

## How leader-based replication works

In <Term slug="leader-based-replication">leader-based replication</Term>, one of the replica
nodes is designated the leader. Write requests always go through this leader, which applies
the change locally and then ships the change (an update log or stream of events) to the other
replicas, called followers. Followers apply what they receive and stay in step with the
leader.

Reads are typically allowed against either the leader or any follower, which is what makes the
"scale out read throughput" motivation above possible. Writes, on the other hand, stay funneled
through a single leader, so this setup alone does not solve write scaling (spreading writes
across multiple leaders is covered in a later lesson).

<Callout type="info">
More followers means more read capacity, but the leader also has to keep streaming its change
log to every one of them, so the leader's distribution cost grows along with the follower
count.
</Callout>

## The synchronous vs. asynchronous trade-off

How long the leader waits before responding to the client — after sending a change to its
followers — changes the system's behavior significantly.

With <Term slug="synchronous-replication">synchronous replication</Term>, the leader waits
until at least one follower confirms it has applied the change before responding to the
client. Because the confirmation is in hand, a write is unlikely to be lost even if the leader
fails right afterward — that follower already has it. The downside is that a slow or
unresponsive follower can block the write entirely.

With <Term slug="asynchronous-replication">asynchronous replication</Term>, the leader responds
to the client without waiting for followers. Write latency stays low and predictable regardless
of follower state, but if the leader fails right after responding, any changes that had not yet
reached a follower can be lost.

<Callout type="warn">
Requiring synchronous confirmation from every follower sounds safe, but a single slow or
unavailable follower then stalls all writes. In practice, teams often use a middle ground —
synchronous confirmation from just one follower with the rest asynchronous — or lean on
asynchronous replication while being explicit about how much data loss is acceptable.
</Callout>

Neither approach is universally correct; the choice is a trade-off, specific to each system,
between the risk of losing a write and the latency/availability cost of avoiding that risk.
