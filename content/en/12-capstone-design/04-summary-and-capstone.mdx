# Summary and the Capstone Exercise

Across the previous three lessons, we looked at how to design and verify
a data system built from multiple components, through the three lenses of
[unbundling](./01-unbundling), [end-to-end correctness](./02-correctness),
and [auditability](./03-auditability). In this final lesson, we move on to
an exercise that pulls together everything from the course so far
(reliability, data models, storage, encoding, replication, partitioning,
transactions, the troubles with distributed systems, consistency and
consensus, batch processing, and stream processing) into a single design
decision.

## Tying the knowledge together

Each of the building blocks covered in this course stands on its own, but
real-world system design always requires choosing several of them at
once.

- Your choice of **replication approach** (module 5) shifts which is
  easier to favor: write availability or read consistency
- Your choice of **partitioning strategy** (module 6) shifts which is
  easier to favor: ease of load distribution or the efficiency of range
  queries
- Your choice of **consistency model** (module 9) shifts whether the
  system, when a partition happens, "stops writes to protect
  consistency" or "keeps accepting writes and tolerates temporary
  inconsistency"

These three choices aren't independent — they influence each other. For
example, if you want strong consistency, that pairs well with a
replication approach that funnels writes through a single place; if you
prioritize availability instead, you'd look at combining it with an
approach that accepts writes concurrently in multiple places. There's no
single "right answer" — the stance that's been consistent throughout this
course, choosing tradeoffs against your requirements, holds all the way to
the end.

## About the capstone exercise

<Callout type="tip">
  The capstone exercise presents the requirements for a service with users
  across multiple regions, and has you choose among a replication
  approach, a partitioning strategy, and a consistency model in a
  branching scenario. Depending on the combination you choose, you get
  feedback on how well that design meets the requirements — and what it
  costs you.
</Callout>

With the tradeoff thinking from this lesson in mind, let's actually put it
into practice and make some design decisions.

## This module's hands-on exercise

Separately from the capstone, this module's own exercise has you implement
the "audit log integrity check" covered in the auditability lesson as
code. By writing logic that detects gaps and duplicates in a sequence of
event numbers, you'll get a feel for confirming the foundation that
supports derived-data reproducibility — namely, that the log itself is
trustworthy and complete.
